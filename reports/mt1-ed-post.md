The final exam is cumulative, so you still have the chance to show mastery of these topics. Here are the three things that cost the class the most points, and exactly where to practice each one.

1. **Proving a divisibility "if and only if."** Problem 30(a) asked you to prove that $18 \mid n$ if and only if $2 \mid n$ and $9 \mid n$ (the numbers depended on your version). It averaged 57%, and only 4 of you earned full credit. One direction is quick: if $n = 18k$, then $n = 2(9k) = 9(2k)$. The other direction is where the work is. It is *not* enough to say "2 and 9 both divide $n$, so their product does." Part (b) shows why: $4 \mid 36$ and $18 \mid 36$, but $72 \nmid 36$. You have to use the particular numbers. For example, write $n = 9b$. Since $n$ and $8b$ are both even, $b = n - 8b$ is even, so $b = 2c$ and $n = 18c$. Practice: Homework 2, Problem 4(b) proves both directions of an "if and only if," and Problem 2(d) and (e) are divisibility claims that sound right but fail.

2. **Proving a set identity.** Problem 31(a) asked you to prove an identity such as $A \cap (B - C) = (A \cap B) - (A \cap C)$. It averaged 67%, and about a third of you earned full credit. A complete proof shows *both* inclusions: take an element of the left side and show it is in the right side, then the reverse. Justify each step with the definition of $\cap$, $\cup$, or $-$. A Venn diagram is a good way to guess whether an identity is true, but it isn't a proof. Practice: Homework 3, Problem 3(b) (De Morgan's law for sets) and Problem 4(a). Redo them from start to finish without notes, writing out both inclusions.

3. **Counting "exactly one."** Question 5 gave the number of Mets fans, Jets fans, and fans of both (on version A, 55, 35, and 5), and asked how many are fans of *exactly one* team. Fewer than half of you got it right. Inclusion–exclusion, $55 + 35 - 5 = 85$, counts the fans of *at least* one team, which includes the 5 who like both. Exactly one is Mets only plus Jets only: $(55 - 5) + (35 - 5) = 80$. Draw the Venn diagram and fill in the middle first. Practice: Homework 3, Problem 1(a)(iii) counts bytes whose second *or* fourth digit is 1. Once you have that, also count the bytes where *exactly one* of those two digits is 1.
